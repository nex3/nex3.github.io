I've been meaning to reblog this since it went up (and indeed I linked to it
from [my own foray into this discussion]). It's a statement of purpose: a clear
ethical line at a time when people are succumbing to learned helplessness and
the ever-present seduction of going along with the herd. People have been saying
since LLMs began picking up steam that questions of their usefulness were beside
the point[^useless], but Bruno is one of the first people I've seen walk that
through to the logical conclusion that we must take up our side of the fight
against them. Over the past week, in my own struggles with LLM mania in culture
and at work, I've looked back to this post as a much-needed source of moral
courage.

[my own foray into this discussion]: https://backloggd.com/u/nex3/review/5546499/

[^useless]: Although, to be clear, there's still good evidence that LLMs provide
    little to no net utility for the vast majority of tasks they're put to. I do
    think this is worth reiterating now and then because it helps to highlight
    the reality that their sudden omnipresence is an act of class war, not rank
    pragmatism.

Four days later, Bruno was among the signatories of [a principled statement]
from a group of IGF jurists against LLMs and their use in games, signaling a
categorical unwillingness "to vote for any game that uses generative AI in any
capacity". We now know that this was catalyzed by a long and arduous internal
discussion about *Big Walk*'s use of LLMs in development; it seems very likely
in retrospect that this discussion was also at the front of his mind when he
wrote the piece above.

[a principled statement]: https://bsky.app/profile/austinwalker.bsky.social/post/3mzvyqaxnzc2w

Damon Reece writes not so much to argue the cause of nuance as to describe how
to find it. They don't equivocate for a moment about the harm that was done, but
they still make room to acknowledge the things House House did right. Above all,
they center the material goal of fighting back against LLMs. It's important to
remember that part of how we regain ground in this fight is by making it
possible for people (or indie game companies) who have made mistakes to come
back to the right side without being forever branded with a scarlet letter.

That is, of course, contingent on them acknowledging the harm they've caused and
taking steps to avoid doing so again. House House hasn't really done this yet,
and while I'm hopefully that they will, it must be acknowledged that part of
what rankles about this isn't just the LLM use but how they handled it before
the situation became public.

This point by Laura Michet is particularly keen. I suspect that at some point
before all this blew up, House House convinced whatever internal elements did
regret the use of LLMs that there was no way to fix it without being
performative[^performative], and so nothing *should* be done. But, as Laura
points out, this is wrongheaded: performance is itself communicative.

[^performative]: I would not be at all surprised if the specific word
    "performative" was used, although that's complete guesswork.

In Judaism, when someone breaks a law "bein adam la'makom", between themself and
G-d[^chavero], they atone for it ritually. This ritual is a kind of performance,
but it has a real effect: it emphasizes to the community and to the person in
question that, despite their lapse, they still consider themselves bound by the
law and part of the community defined by it. It also creates a clear dividing
line after which they can set aside the infraction and move on.

[^chavero]: This includes things like dietary laws, keeping shabbos, and so on.
    The other category of law is "bein adam la'chavero", between themself and
    another. These require working with the harmed party to repair the damage
    done.

Even if House House had decided against the use of LLMs well after the deed was
done and the generated functions had been committed over a dozen times, they
could have deleted some chunk of code around them and hired a contractor to fill
in the gaps by hand. In fact, they still could! Would this be performative? Of
course. But that performance is itself meaningful.

This isn't the only place where people seem to struggle with the idea that
cultural actions can be appropriate to address cultural problems.

Mike Cook articulates some of the points I tried to make much more thoroughly
than I was able to. I think a lot of folks envision the way people interact with
the world as fundamentally mechanistic, where every piece has to line up just so
in order for anything to work. In their conception, rules don't work unless
they're rigorously enforceable; actions whose purpose is in the doing rather
than what's left after it's done are performative and thus invalid.

In reality, people interact with the world more like an ecology[^ecology]. We
are interconnected in ways far too complex to ever treat as fully deterministic,
but still comprehensible enough to be amenable to thoughtful intervention. That
intervention works best when we think of the whole system and frame our actions
as communication (of regret, of expectations) rather than blunt mechanism.

[^ecology]: I'm using this as a metaphor here but it's always worth remembering
    that it's also literally true!

Mike's post ends on a dour note, one that I admit resonates with me as well.
I've always found it hard to persuade people of things I believe in strongly;
when something feels so self-evidently wrong in such manifold ways as LLMs, I
don't know how to connect the dots for someone who doesn't feel the same way. So
I try to trust those who better-suited to diplomacy than I to shoulder that
burden. And instead, on this blog and in my day-to-day life, I do my best help
clarify and articulate my position for the benefit of those who feel it as
strongly as I do but don't quite have the words for it yet.

Austin Walker shares his own articulations of the problem, written as part of
the original IGF discussion. He makes the important point that LLMs are one part
of a systemic assault, a last-ditch campaign of resource extraction from a
capitalist class running out of [colonies to pillage]. He draws the frame of
this conflict out to cover the system in which it exists, the ways it intersects
with race and class, the way it defines and simultaneously is defined by the
agency of those caught up in it.

[colonies to pillage]: https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1916/imp-hsc/

He wrote this originally for IGF judges and jurists in a consummate act of
diplomacy. But even though I'm not involved with the IGF, and by odds you
probably aren't either, we have agency as well. We can lift up our voices in our
own little spheres of influence and say "this is what we consider unacceptable."

And we full circle back to Bruno again. This time he's writing explicitly about
*Big Walk* and the IGF, but I want to bring your attention particularly to his
description of reacting to LLMs as a question of ethics. I tend towards a
functionalist approach most of the time, focusing more on the question of "what
strategy do we employ to achieve our goals", but those goals ultimately spring
from ethical convictions. Bruno's logic is simple and pointed: if you have the
conviction that needlessly causing harm is wrong, then you must condemn that act
here; and if you don't condemn that act, then you're mistaken about your
convictions.

This harm certainly was needless—as Damon, Laura, and Bruno all point out, the
industry is flooded with programmers who have the expertise to solve these
problems and who need work due to awful hiring conditions caused by exactly
LLMs. As Austin and Bruno explain in detail, the harm is also structural,
participating in and helping to prop up a system that's inimical to everything
good about art, up to and including the art in *Big Walk* itself.

But without letting go of all that fire and fury, I still want to end this by
echoing Damon in saying that there *is* a path back, for House House and for
anyone else who has used LLMs in the past but is starting to understand how
corrosive they really are. Express regret, and commit to doing better. Be a
little performative if it helps reaffirm that you stand with your community, not
with the tech oligarchs. The future is not set in stone, and even if it were,
it's far better to go out fighting.
