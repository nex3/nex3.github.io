I decided to rise to this challenge by loading up the list of all games I've
ever played on Backloggd, sorting it from least to most popular, and finding the
first one for which I felt I had something worth saying that I hadn't already
articulated on my old media journal. What I ended up finding was a game I'd
played long before I ever thought of tracking my impressions of media. It's
sadly now unplayable due to the creeping march of time, but its place in my
history and memory is worth writing about all the same:

  <p>This was one of a handful of games that my friends and I would play
  endlessly on the school library computers during lunch in middle school. It,
  more than anything in an actual cabinet, informed my taste for what "arcadey"
  games should be: fast-paced, easy to jump back into after a death, with just
  enough sense of progression and buildcraft to make each attempt feel a bit
  different than the one before.</p>

  <p>It's a damn shame that this isn't playable anymore (I tried both
  applet-to-WASM plugins to running appletviewer from an old JDK), because I'd
  love to revisit some of the design decisions with older eyes. Instead, I'll
  have to make do with decades-old memories of power-ups changing shot shape and
  damage. In retrospect none of this is that different than any of its
  contemporary STGs, a genre which I wasn't even totally unfamiliar with at the
  time—I certainly had access to at least one cab on occasion, although I can't
  remember which game it held.</p>
  
  <p>But <em>Javaders</em> had the crucial difference of being freely available
  on the web, even if it was much rougher around the edges, so I could throw
  myself against it again and again. This too is crucial to the arcade
  experience, as <a href="https://backloggd.com/u/nex3/review/2313903/"><em>UFO
  50</em></a> taught me (or perhaps re-taught me after I forgot the lesson I'd
  first learned from <em>Javaders</em> itself). The game is as much the
  progression through one's own skill as it is the progression through the
  game-space, or rather it's the dialectical relationship between the two. I
  don't remember for sure, but I think I did see the end of this game back in
  the day. I certainly put in the time on this game and came away with a
  fondness for it that's lasted all these years.</p>
