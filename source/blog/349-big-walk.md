  <p>Yesterday <a
  href="https://www.resetera.com/threads/indie-darling-of-the-year-big-walk-used-ai-for-consultation-during-development-disclosed-to-igf-judges.1683377/"
  >it came out</a> that <em>Big Walk</em> used LLMs in its development. In <a
  href="https://bigwalk.game/faq/#ai">House House's response</a> (<a
  href="https://web.archive.org/web/20261110064927/https://bigwalk.game/faq/#ai"
  >archived here</a >), they stated that the use was small and largely
  consultative rather than generative, although some amount of LLM-generated
  code was used as the basis for code that shipped.</p>

  <blockquote><em>Big Walk</em> was developed without an internal AI usage
  policy in place, and we consider that lack of policy a failure. We will work
  to have a more comprehensive policy going forwards.</blockquote>

  <p>Reading between the lines, this final paragraph suggests that LLM usage was
  not a matter of company policy but rather one or a few engineers making
  individual decisions about their workflows. It also <u>implies</u> that they
  will institute a policy against this in the future, but leaves open the
  possibility that the policy they settle on will allow some amount of LLM
  usage.</p>

  <p>I'm sure there's a tremendous amount of bickering about this happening on
  social media; I'm no longer in that particular torment nexus, so I don't know
  the details. I do know that this news will affect how I think about <em>Big
  Walk</em> going forward, so I think it's worth taking the time to revise my
  review and describe how and why my feelings have changed. In doing so, I hope
  to articulate my position on LLM usage more broadly.</p>

  <p>I loved my time with <em>Big Walk</em>. I don't hand out five-star reviews
  lightly, but I was confident this game would earn one as soon as I began to
  understand just how cleverly it created new forms of play and puzzle solving
  between friends. It was clearly a work of masterful artistry, painted with the
  palette of real human interaction which is tremendously difficult to master
  but intensely rewarding to experience. That artistry was real, and it is still
  present regardless of the tools used.</p>

  <p>At the same time, no piece of art exists in isolation. All art is in
  conversation with the culture that created it, with the art that surrounded
  and preceded it, with the economic conditions under which it was produced. If
  history is driven by material contradictions, as Marx and Engels taught, then
  we can expect to see aspects of that contradictory tension in the artifacts
  produced during that history. Within <em>Big Walk</em>, an artifact of
  impressive artistry, we see the use of a tool whose very existence is inimical
  to art as a discipline, a tool built from the work of artists and craftspeople
  over their vehement objections, a tool whose creators depend on the
  immiseration of workers for their own success, a tool whose effects on the
  physical world are profoundly detrimental.</p>

  <p>Knowing that LLMs were used doesn't remove the artistry from <em>Big
  Walk</em>, but it changes everything about its conversation with the culture
  around it. <em>Big Walk</em> is now, indelibly, in some part an attack on the
  artistry of all the games that came before it and on the very possibility of
  all the games that could come after. Even House House's response gestures at
  this:</p>

  <blockquote>We have some concern that voluntary disclosures like this might
  contribute to the normalisation of this technology, or its treatment as
  “inevitable”. We support the work of those who are trying to limit the harm
  generative AI is doing to videogames and our world, and apologise that <em>Big
  Walk</em>’s LLM usage may not meet their standards.</blockquote>

  <p>Of course, the best way not to contribute to the normalization of LLMs is
  <em>not to use them at all</em>. But I suspect by the time any discussions
  were being had at a company-wide level, the horses had fled the stable and
  locking the door would do no good. I truly do not envy them the position of
  knowing people would view all their work as tainted by a few decisions made in
  the heat of debugging, and that there was no real way to undo the results of
  those decisions.</p>

  <p>Some may sympathize so much with this dilemma that they take it as a sign
  that people are speaking out too harshly against LLMs. I could not disagree
  more with this position. On the contrary, we must do everything we can to make
  the use of LLMs culturally radioactive, such that <em>any</em> use in a
  cultural product is treated as putting that product in conflict with human
  culture as a whole. It doesn't need to be black or white—we can acknowledge
  that <em>Big Walk</em> is far less in conflict than in the mountains of
  steamslop that continue to pile up around us—but we need to be clear that the
  only amount of LLM use that's truly acceptable is none. While we can grant
  some grace to those who don't yet realize that <a
  href="https://azhdarchid.com/there-are-sides/" >this is a conflict and there
  are sides</a>, we must in the final accounting recognize that works on the
  wrong side do real harm that we cannot paper over.</p>

  <p>Others may point out that condemning all use of LLMs incentivizes lying
  about whether a game uses them, to which I reply: good! If someone is going to
  do something broadly (not specifically) harmful, they <em>should</em> hide it
  in shame and fear that the world might one day know. It can be hard to
  remember in the brazen 2020s, but shame works as a deterrent. Forcing
  undesirable behaviors underground mitigates their spread! We've all seen this
  happening in reverse: as it became more feasible for trans people to exist in
  public over the last few decades, an explosion of people realized they could
  transition too. We can and must harness this power to drive LLM use into the
  shadows. Responsible creators will disclose the use when asked even if it
  stands to harm them, as House House did. And irresponsible ones will be forced
  to balance whatever they think they stand to gain by standing on the necks of
  artists against the very concrete cost of scandal if it comes to light.</p>

  <p>In most of the world today, the people don't exercise political power. Good
  work is being done to try and change that, but it will not change tomorrow nor
  will it change next year. But we can exercise power through culture because,
  despite capitalist attempts to capture it completely, part of culture
  <em>is</em> the people. When enough of us speak in one voice and say "this is
  not what we want art to be", the world will listen.</p>
