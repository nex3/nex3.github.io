  <p>I've never seriously played an MMORPG. I played maybe an hour of <em>World
  of Warcraft</em> during its heyday at a friend's urging, but found it
  immediately boring. Nor am I much for shooters, especially in a competitive
  context; I played a bit of <em>Halo</em> and <em>Halo 2</em> multiplayer when
  they were the biggest thing in video games, and enjoyed myself, but I never
  <em>got good</em> and I certainly didn't feel the drive to hone my skills on
  the grindstone of Xbox Live.</p>

  <p>But I've been playing <em>Marathon</em>. I'm still playing
  <em>Marathon</em>. I would have written this very review months earlier,
  except that I was too busy playing <u>Marathon</u>.</p>

  <p>It's a surprise that I like this so much. Even my girlfriend who loves
  trying to push me out of my video game comfort zone almost as much as she
  loves <em>Marathon</em> (both the 90s and 2020s incarnations) didn't bother
  encouraging me to play it. But I had a gap in my gaming schedule and an itch
  to play something with a sense of both action and progression, so I gave it a
  shot and was blown away by what I found.</p>

  <p><em>Marathon</em> clearly isn't an MMO. There's no persistent game world,
  even in the <em>Destiny</em> "shared-world" sense, and the game progression is
  entirely based on the items you bring back from short-lived runs. But my more
  controversial contention is that <strong><em>Marathon</em> isn't entirely a
  shooter</strong>, either.</p>

  <p>Certainly, for those drawn to FPS team fights, this game delivers in
  spades. I'm told by aficionados of the genre that the guns have "the Bungie
  sauce". The character-shooter-style shells all play distinctly while remaining
  balanced among each other. The level design allows for hugely diverse
  engagements with lots of opportunities to play the map for advantage rather
  than relying on raw aim fundamentals.</p>

  <p>But even here I'm betraying my own biases, because my aim fundamentals are
  still weak. This game has been driving me to get better, because no part of it
  is fully separable from the whole, but I've got a lot of practice to do before
  I can go toe-to-toe with people who have been playing <em>Overwatch</em> and
  <em>Fortnite</em> and <em>Apex</em> for years. The places I make up the
  difference, and which keep drawing me into the game even 800 hours in, are
  those in which you can shift the focus away from raw shooting and towards the
  rest of the game.</p>

  <p>Here is <em>Marathon</em>'s fundamental brilliance (and possibly that of
  the nascent extraction shooter genre as a whole): by making the fundamental
  goal retaining and accumulating loot, which is distinct from but in dialog
  with fighting other people, it produces complex and intense emergent
  experiences of a sort I rarely see in games.</p>

  <p>As an example, <em>Marathon</em> is the most effective survival horror game
  I've ever played in terms of making me <em>actually emotionally afraid</em>.
  As much as I love the mechanical genius of <a rel="nofollow" target="_blank"
  href="https://backloggd.com/u/nex3/review/194159/"><em>Resident Evil</em></a>
  or the writing of <a rel="nofollow" target="_blank"
  href="https://backloggd.com/u/nex3/review/2055307/"><em>Silent Hill
  2</em></a>, neither of them made me feel terror. Playing <em>Marathon</em>,
  hiding in the tunnels with the loot I stole from a real human team, listening
  to their footsteps over my head as they search for me, knowing that all that
  stood between me and my demise was my own ability to outthink and outmaneuver
  them—then, I felt terror.</p>

  <p>As another example, <em>Marathon</em> is a game about knowing the terrain.
  I was in a firefight through a doorway, accompanied by two teammates who were
  no more confident at raw gunplay than I. We'd fought to a standstill, and I
  told them to hold the door and keep the enemy's attention. I crept through a
  vent, knowing it would lead me to a platform just above the other team, where
  I rained gunfire upon them, breaking the stalemate and yielding us a victory
  against superior fighters with better gear. This is a game about playing
  smart, and on my best days, I can be smart.</p>

  <p>To play <em>Marathon</em> is to play a hundred different games at once.
  It's an FPS, sure, but it's also survival horror, it's stealth action, it's a
  puzzle game, it's a game about navigation, it's a game about psychogeography,
  and on rare occasions it's even a game about making friends. The game is
  carefully structured so that stories unfold as part of the play, and I find
  that as fascinating to witness as an art form as it is fun to engage in as a
  player.</p>

  <p>I don't know how much longer this game will be around. The industry doesn't
  seem to be doing well, Bungie is certainly not thriving under its Sony
  overlords, and <em>Marathon</em> will never be the money fountain that
  <em>Destiny</em> was in its heyday (even <em>Destiny</em> for all its success
  is now officially over). I'm just glad I'm getting to experience this while
  it's here, while the lobbies are still filled with players, before this
  beautiful moment fades into memory and lore.</p>
