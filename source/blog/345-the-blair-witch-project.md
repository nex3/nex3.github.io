  <p>I was surprised at how much this felt... genuine, even now. The acting is
  superb -- the kids really feel like kids, in the same sort of over-the-top
  stilted way that the kids in <em>Texas Chainsaw Massacre</em> are, and if you
  know me you know how much TCM means to me. It's extremely
  high praise.</p>

  <p>So much of the horror in here is in the audio, in the tension
  <em>implied</em> by the audio. The woods remind me of the house in TCM --
  forever and nowhere all at once. Also similar to TCM is the tension
  <em>between</em> the natural and the constructed.</p>

  <blockquote><p>"You going to write us a happy ending, Heather?"</p>

  </blockquote><p>Horror movies are in many ways an interactive experience
  between the viewer and the film. I'm not the first one to say that, obviously,
  but there are some horror movies that take that to another level, and this is
  one of them, I think.</p>

  <p>If the conditions are right, <em>you</em> become part of the movie too --
  you are hunting through the static film, you are searching through the blurry
  woods, you are listening to every slight sound in the soundtrack. You are
  looking for the <em>constructed</em> among the <em>natural</em>.</p>

  <p>a lot of this movie feels like it's fixated on the idea of why the movie
  <em>existed</em>. there's an in-built question there, because this is "found
  footage", thus <em>someone</em> "found" it. But Heather is filming because she
  has to film. All the kids are filming because they <em>have to film</em>. They
  have to. Even as they're slowly losing their mind, being tortured by
  everything around you, being tortured by the endless woods.</p>

  <blockquote><p>"All I thought was -- I had to get it. I had to get it
  on 16."</p>

  </blockquote><p>Because why? Because we need to see more. Because we need to
  see what else there is. Because they are kids who are trying to find something
  horrific, and that has an inherent voyeuristic thrill attached to it. I think
  this movie is trying to communicate that you too are voyeuristic, that you
  <em>too</em> want to be thrilled.</p>

  <p>You become just as much a participant in the hunt for the Blair Witch as
  any of the kids are. You are also hunting and being hunted. You are both
  terrified and obsessed with seeing something that you can put a finger on and
  say "yes, this was <em>definitely</em> constructed."</p>

  <p>You want to see the blood, you want to see if there really is something at
  the center of all the tension. You want to see, even now, if maybe it
  <em>is</em> real, or maybe at least it's real <em>enough</em> to freak
  you out.</p>

Not particularly timely, but I reread Dante's review after watching _The Blair
With Project_ myself and found myself nodding vigorously along. His insight that
the *lack* of overt horror draws the viewer into becoming a participant in
nervously searching for the wrongness they know must exist is something I feel
particularly keenly. It isn't all of it, but this is certainly a big part of why
I feel like horror is usually strongest the less it shows the monster.
